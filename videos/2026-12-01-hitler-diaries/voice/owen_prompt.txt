[matter of fact] Then he starts hand-writing notebooks, and claims they're Hitler's secret diaries. He sells them under a fake name, through a middleman. [conversational] He talks his bosses into paying about nine million marks. Marks were West Germany's money back then, and this was a fortune. And Heidemann? He secretly kept a big chunk for himself. In April nineteen eighty-three, the magazine tells the whole world. But nobody really checked the most obvious thing, the cover. On the covers were plastic letters from a shop, F and H. But Hitler's first name, Adolf, starts with an A. It should have said A H. [playfully] Send this to the friend who never proofreads anything. [matter of fact] Then Germany's national archives tested the paper and the ink. Both were modern, way too new to be real. And that took less than two weeks after the announcement. [seriously] In nineteen eighty-five, a court in Hamburg convicted Kujau and Heidemann. Each was sentenced to about four and a half years in prison.
