## The one idea behind all of it

A hook is a plain promise with layers added. Robert's caption says it: "Good hook = adding layers to strengthen question". Both layering reels climb the same ladder, and each rung is one added ingredient:

1. plain value ("how to X")
2. a bold claim or a time limit ("the best way", "in 60 seconds")
3. a payoff the viewer wants ("people never scroll past")
4. a number ("3 ways")
5. proof you earned it ("I tested 30")
6. a contrast with what most people do, or a story question ("is it possible to...?")

Our hook drafts should be built the same way: write the plain version, then add layers until it cannot be ignored, then cut it back to 12 words (gate S1).

## Top 10 hook formulas

Each has the source, a fill-in template for each series, and two example hooks. All examples are 12 words or fewer and none opens with a soft "What if...?".

### 1. Proof number first
Source: Robert, "I tested 30 hooks and these are the 3 things..."
- Decoded: `[Who counted] counted [number] [things]. [Number] were [fake/rigged].`
- What If: `I ran [the choice] [number] times. [Result].`
- "I played Monty Hall 1,000 times. Switching won 667."
- "Experts opened his rare wine. They found cheap wine inside." *(Decoded: verify)*

### 2. Huge number, then "but"
Source: Robert, "Our brand got 400 million views in the last 6 months, but we decided not to drop once."
- Decoded: `He [had/won/sold] [huge number], but [the one thing that makes no sense].`
- What If: `[Huge result] from [tiny start], but [the catch].`
- "He picked a sixteen million dollar jackpot. He could never cash it." *(verify)*
- "A penny doubled for 30 days beats five million dollars."

### 3. The pitch, then "that was a lie"
Source: Robert, 14 s of brags, then "We're lying. None of that is true."
- Decoded: `[The con's pitch, stated flat]. None of it was true.`
- What If: `[The answer everyone gives]. That answer is wrong.`
- "He said he was a Saudi prince. He was from Michigan." *(verify)*
- "Two briefcases left, so it's fifty-fifty. That's wrong."
- Rule 16 check: the false line must be labelled as the pitch on screen (a PITCH stamp), so we never state a false fact as ours.

### 4. Most people vs the few
Source: Wang, "Most people study 8 hours a day for B's, but here's 3 things I do..."
- Decoded: `Most [victims] checked [the obvious thing]. Nobody checked [the tell].`
- What If: `Most people take [the safe option]. It pays [x] less.`
- "Most people take the million. The penny pays five times more."
- "Everyone checked the label. Nobody checked the year on it." *(verify)*

### 5. Turn the claim into a story question
Source: Wang's top rung: "Is it possible to get straight A's in pre-med studying just 3 hours a day?"
- Decoded: `Can one [ordinary person] [impossible thing] with only [small tool]?`
- What If: `Could you [do the thing] with only [limit]?`
- "Can one man rig the lottery with one line of code?" *(verify)*
- "Could you carry your own weight in twenty-dollar bills?"
- Note: this is a hard question with a number in it, not a soft "what if". It passes S1 only if the stakes are in the sentence.

### 6. Shrink the time
Source: Wang, "in 60 seconds" and "4 years of X in 60 seconds".
- Decoded: `[Years] of fraud, explained in [seconds].`
- What If: `[Long span] of [money thing], in [seconds].`
- "Eight years of fake wine, explained in ninety seconds." *(verify the years)*
- "Thirty days of doubling a penny, in sixty seconds."

### 7. Superlative plus a name
Source: Kallaway, "This is the best storytelling hack... It's called comprehension maxing."
- Decoded: `This is the [dumbest/boldest] [con] ever convicted. It's called [name].`
- What If: `This is the [worst/best] deal in [place]. It's called [name].`
- "This is the laziest royal scam ever convicted. Fake prince." *(verify; "laziest" is our opinion, keep it clearly ours)*
- "This is the worst bet in the casino. Everyone takes it."

### 8. "The reason your X looks like this"
Source: Kallaway at 5.2 s, pointing at a falling chart that is about the viewer.
- Decoded: `The reason [victims] wired him money is [one plain cause].`
- What If: `The reason your [savings/paycheck] looks like this is [cause].`
- "The reason your gut picks the wrong briefcase is one number."
- "The reason millionaires paid him: he ordered for them." *(verify)*

### 9. Humiliation in the first sentence
Source: Robert's most-viewed Short: "she told me my fit was trash and broke up with me on the spot."
- Decoded: `[Powerful person] got fooled by [something embarrassing].`
- What If: `You'd lose [thing] in [short time] and feel smart doing it.`
- "Billionaires got fooled by a man with a fake business card." *(verify)*
- "You'd turn down five million dollars and feel smart doing it."
- Decoded limit: mock the con and the tactic, never the victims (STYLE.md). Aim the humiliation at the con artist's sloppiness where possible.

### 10. The climbing counter
Source: both layering reels; the counter chip (100, 1K, 10K, 100K, 1M, 10M) is the hook.
- Decoded: `[Small first take] ... [counter climbs] ... [total stolen].` First line names the start: `It started with [small number].`
- What If: `Day 1: [tiny]. Day [n]: [huge].`
- "Day one, a penny. Day thirty, five million dollars."
- "It started with one fake bottle. It ended at millions." *(verify)*
- This is our existing "The Counter" format; the new part is putting the counter on screen from frame 0 and making it jump every 3 s.

Runners-up: a rule you get in one second plus a visible score (his game Shorts), and deadline plus open question ("in 10 days... will it pay off?", his 124K-view video).

## Explanation techniques, mapped to "explain at kindergarten level" (gate S10b)

| What they do | Where | Our rule it serves | How we do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Say the same sentence again with one thing added | Robert, Wang | ONE new idea per line | In the "how it worked" step, repeat the sentence and add one piece each time. |
| Name the idea in two or three words | Kallaway ("visual alignment", "hawk eye narratives") | every term explained in plain words | Give each con trick a kid-level name ("the label swap", "the fake uncle"), then explain it. |
| The picture shows the exact word being said | Kallaway: bar drains on "losing", bird dives on "dive" | walk it step by step | Storyboard word by word. If the voice says "doubled", the pile doubles on that word. |
| Broad first, then narrow | Kallaway's hawk | who / what / why before the numbers | First 10 s: who, what he wanted, why anyone believed him. Numbers only after. |
| One everyday analogy per hard idea, then map it back | Kallaway: hawk, then cone labelled "setup context" and "point" | every big number gets an everyday comparison | One comparison per number, shown as a picture, then say what it stands for. |
| A map of the parts before the parts | Kallaway: boxes 1 and 2; Robert: "3 major things" | nothing a viewer must already know | Show "2 steps" or "3 tells" as boxes, then open them one at a time. |
| A list that builds one line at a time | Robert 003 Short | no 3 bare numbers in a row | Never show a full list at once. |
| One prop per point | Robert: whiteboard, clothes stack, truck icon | step by step | One object per step in the con (the bottle, the stamp, the wire slip). |
| Label the technique on screen | Robert, Wang: label above the head | plain words | A small plain-word tag per beat ("THE PITCH", "THE TRICK", "THE CATCH"). |
| Name the common mistake | Kallaway: "too many people jump too deep too soon" | a 10-year-old must follow | One line: "Here's where everyone gets lost." Then slow down. |

## Marketing, CTA and caption patterns

- **The follow ask is part of the content.** In both layering reels the last rung of the lesson is the follow. It lands as a joke and a payoff, not a plea.
- **The ask is written from the viewer's side.** Wang: "I'm a grower, so I follow..." Robert's brand: "join our community of people who take action". Following means joining a group you want to be in.
- **One spoken ask per short video.** Robert and Wang: one. Kallaway: one, at 84%. Nobody asks for a like out loud.
- **The comment keyword is the engine.** Wang (keyword in caption): comments are 11.7% of likes. Robert (no keyword): 0.55%. Kallaway (keyword spoken and shown): 212 comments against 401 likes on day one, and the visible comments are just the word "Story".
- **The caption does a second job.** Robert: one-line takeaway + 5 hashtags. Wang: the offer first, one line of value, "Good luck!". Kallaway: the whole script, then the asks, then 5 hashtags. All three use 5 hashtags or fewer.
- **On YouTube Shorts the title is the CTA** ("join the email list for 10% off!"), and the product or payoff fills the last 5 to 10 s with no talking.
- **A tagline repeated everywhere.** "Take action" is in his bio, his sign-offs and on the clothes.

For us (inside RULES.md): keep like at ~30%, send at ~55%, follow at ~80% as a promise, comment question after the payoff, no end CTA block, no "link in bio". What changes is the wording and the comment question; see changes 4 and 5.

## Why reel 2 works (Martin Wang, "a great video")

- **Hook:** it starts mid-lesson. No "today I'll teach you". The counter chip reading "100" is a promise that it will climb, and the first climb happens at 1.0 s.
- **Explanation:** one sentence, said six times, with one ingredient added each time and the ingredient named on screen. You can hear the difference between a weak hook and a strong one without any theory. The outfits escalate with the counter, so you get the point with the sound off.
- **CTA:** the follow is the top rung of the ladder, at the 10.5M mark, phrased as the viewer's own thought. The caption trades a free list for a one-word comment, which produced 4,981 comments.
- **For us:** this is a ready-made shape for a What If ("same bet, one change at a time, watch the number climb") and for a Decoded ("same lie, one layer at a time, watch the take climb").

## Why reel 3 works (Kallaway, "an ad but it has good info")

- **Hook:** a superlative, then a name for the idea, then a chart that is about you, all inside 10 s. Three reasons to stay before any teaching.
- **Explanation:** it does what it teaches. Pictures match the spoken word to the second, and it goes broad to narrow: name, problem, a two-box map, then each box with one example or one analogy. The pinned section title means you are never lost.
- **Persuasion structure:** problem (your chart falls), cause (viewers lose understanding), named cure, two steps, borrowed authority (a clip of a famous creator, "his team spends months"), the common mistake, then the offer. The value comes first and is complete on its own, so the offer feels earned.
- **CTA:** held until 84%, one action, one word to type, with the reward shown as nine page thumbnails.
- **Ad-specific, do NOT copy:**
  - the lead magnet and comment-to-DM automation (we have no doc and no DM bot; a keyword that sends nothing is a bait-and-switch);
  - the paid 1:1 coaching ask and "limited spots" scarcity;
  - three stacked asks in the caption;
  - another creator's footage used as the proof (third-party footage; our rules say real public records only);
  - "hack", "cheat code" and "-maxxing" wording, and "feed it into AI";
  - claims we cannot source ("his team spends months").

## What NOT to copy

- His topics (hooks, clothing brand, his story), his lines, his footage, his audio, his view-counter numbers, his profile-card pop-in.
- The fake view counter as if it were real data. Ours must count real things: dollars, days, victims, odds.
- Unsourced superlatives about ourselves ("the best marketer in the world"). Decoded facts stay sourced and attributed.
- The end-of-video follow line. Both layering reels end on the follow; our gate S8 forbids ending on an ask. Take the idea (the ask is part of the story), put it at ~80%.
- Lying as a hook without a label. His "we're lying" works because he confesses in 15 s. For us the false line must be stamped as the con's pitch from the first frame.
- "Welcome to my channel" openings (his lowest-viewed long videos start that way).
- Face-to-camera delivery. We are faceless; the equivalent of his changing rooms and outfits is our storyteller moving and the scene changing.
- 4+ words per second. It breaks S10b and S11.

## The 10 changes to make, most impactful first

1. **Build every hook by layering, and write it down.** For each script, write the plain hook, then add in order: bold claim, payoff, number, proof, contrast or story question. Keep the strongest rung that fits in 12 words. This replaces "write 10 unrelated variants" (S2) with 10 variants that are rungs and remixes of one ladder.
2. **Put a live number on screen from frame 0 and make it jump about every 3 s.** A dollar or day counter chip under the title, climbing by big steps (the take in a Decoded, the pile in a What If). Both layering reels hold attention with nothing else.
3. **Match the picture to the exact spoken word.** Add a column to DIRECTION files: for each line, the one word that triggers the visual change, and what changes. If the picture does not show the word, fix the picture or the line.
4. **Make the comment question a one-word answer.** After the payoff ask for a single word or number ("Switch or stay?", "Penny or million?", "Which tell would you have caught: 1, 2 or 3?"). Put the same question as the first comment. This is the honest version of the keyword trick, with nothing promised in return.
5. **Rewrite the follow ask as part of the story and as belonging.** At ~80%: one clause that is the next beat of the story and names the group ("People who check the math follow Bad Math. Now watch what the judge did."). No "follow for more".
6. **Label the beat on screen.** A small pinned tag that changes with each section: THE PITCH, THE TRICK, THE CATCH, THE MATH, THE SENTENCE. Viewers always know where they are, and each change is a free pattern interrupt.
7. **Go broad to narrow and show the map.** First 10 s after the hook: who, what, why in plain words. Then show the steps as boxes ("2 steps", "3 tells") and open one at a time. This is S10b made visible.
8. **Name every trick like a kid would.** Two or three plain words per mechanism, said and shown before it is explained ("the label swap", "the borrowed crown"). One analogy per hard idea, drawn, then mapped back.
9. **Use "the pitch, then that was a lie" as the default Decoded opening** and "the obvious answer, then that's wrong" for What If. It is our Pitch vs The Math format moved into the first 5 seconds. Stamp the pitch as PITCH.
10. **Change the whole layout on a schedule.** Alternate two layouts (full-scene and split: graphic on top, storyteller below) every 8 to 10 s, on top of the 2 to 3 s micro-changes already required by V4. Keep captions to 1 to 3 words, mid-screen.
