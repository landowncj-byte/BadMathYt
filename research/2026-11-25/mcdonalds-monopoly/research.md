## Story beats (timeline)
1. **1987:** McDonald's starts its Monopoly peel-off game. It outsources the whole thing to a contractor, Simon Marketing.
2. Working in Simon's security department (press: its chief of security) is **Jerome "Uncle Jerry" Jacobson**, an ex-police officer. His job: personally carry the handful of high-value winning pieces to the packaging plants.
3. A supplier accidentally sends him a sheet of the tamper-proof seals used on the piece envelopes. Now he can open an envelope in an airport bathroom, swap the winners out, and reseal it.
4. **1995:** St. Jude Children's Hospital receives a $1 million winning piece in an anonymous envelope. (Jacobson later admitted sending it.) McDonald's pays it out to the hospital anyway.
5. **1995–2000:** Jacobson sells winning pieces for a cut — first to friends and family, then through a man named Jerry Colombo, who even appears in a McDonald's TV commercial as the "winner" of a Dodge Viper.
6. Recruiters find "winners" around the country; the winner pays a kickback up front. Almost all the top prizes for years go to the ring: **more than $24 million**.
7. **2000–01:** a tip reaches the FBI's Jacksonville office. Wiretaps follow the pieces ("Operation Final Answer").
8. **August 21, 2001:** the first eight arrests are announced by the Attorney General. FBI then: more than $13 million in fraudulent prizes; real odds for the public on those prizes — effectively zero.
9. **September 10, 2001:** the trial opens in Jacksonville. The next morning is September 11. The story vanishes from the news.
10. Outcome: 50+ people convicted. Jacobson pleads guilty: **37 months** and **$12.5 million** restitution. McDonald's runs a make-good giveaway and drops Simon.

## Convictions
- **Jerome P. Jacobson** — pleaded guilty (mail fraud; M.D. Fla., Jacksonville). **37 months**, **$12.5M restitution** (2002).
- 50+ others (recruiters and fake winners) convicted, mostly by plea; four convictions reversed on appeal.
- Gennaro "Jerry" Colombo died in a 1998 car crash before the arrests — never charged. Wikipedia describes him as "of the Colombo crime family"; ⚠ that characterisation is disputed in other reporting → say "a man with alleged mob ties" or just "an associate".
- McDonald's: **victim**, not accused.

## On-screen-safe quotes
VERIFIED verbatim in DOJ release #422, Aug 21, 2001 (https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2001/August/422ag.htm):
- Attorney General John Ashcroft: **"This fraud scheme denied McDonald's customers a fair and equal chance of winning."** and **"Those involved in this type of corruption will find out that breaking the law is no game."**
- DOJ: **"More than $13 million worth of grand prizes have been corruptly 'won' by the co-conspirators in the scheme."**
- Also in the release: "McDonald's provided essential assistance in the investigation and has cooperated fully with the FBI" (supports "McDonald's was the victim").
Not verified: the FBI "real odds were really zero" line (CBS summary) — paraphrase only.

## Commonly repeated but NOT verified here — don't say
- "Nobody ever won" — small prizes and food prizes were legitimate; say "the big prizes".
- "McDonald's knew" — no.
- "He stole pieces for 12 years (1989–2001)" — the DOJ complaint says the scheme "began as early as 1995". Use 1995.
- "Colombo was a mafia boss."
- "McDonald's gave away $10 million afterwards" — a make-good giveaway happened; amount not verified here ($10M is the usual figure; Fox says McDonald's "spent $25 million apologizing" — unreconciled).
- "The tipster was a jealous ex / family member" — HBO narrative; not checked.
