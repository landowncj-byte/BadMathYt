## Story beats
1. Early 2000s, Los Angeles: a young collector, Rudy Kurniawan, becomes the biggest buyer and seller of rare wine in America. He opens $10,000 bottles at dinner like water.
2. **2006:** one auction house sells his cellar in two sales — **$10.6 million in January, $24.7 million in October**. The second is a world record for a single-owner wine auction.
3. **April 2008, New York:** another of his consignments includes bottles of Domaine Ponsot **Clos Saint-Denis** with vintages from **1945 to 1971**.
4. The tell: the winemaker, Laurent Ponsot, says his family **didn't start making Clos Saint-Denis until 1982**. He flies to New York and has the lots pulled. On the stand years later: bottles like these "cannot exist".
5. **March 8, 2012:** the FBI arrests Kurniawan at his home in Arcadia, California. Inside: empty old bottles, corks, wax, stamps, and thousands of printed labels. Prosecutors call the house a **wine factory**: he blended older cheap wines with newer ones and poured the mix into old bottles.
6. Billionaire collector Bill Koch tells the jury he paid **$2.1 million for 219 fake bottles**.
7. **December 18, 2013:** convicted by a Manhattan jury (mail and wire fraud). **August 7, 2014: 10 years.** Forfeit **$20 million**, pay back **$28.4 million** to seven victims.
8. Released from prison in **November 2020**, deported to Indonesia in **April 2021**.
9. Payoff: experts with decades of training drank it and praised it. The thing that caught him wasn't taste — it was a date on a label that was 37 years too early (1982 − 1945).

## Story loop (suggested)
STAKES: millionaires paying five figures a bottle; one guy supplying them. · QUESTION: how do you fake a wine that the world's best tasters are drinking? · TWIST: they never caught it by tasting. A winemaker read the label: 1945 — on a wine first made in 1982. · REHOOKS: "the record: $24.7 million in one auction" → "then one bottle said 1945" → "so the FBI opened his kitchen".

## Convictions
- **Rudy Kurniawan** — convicted at trial, Dec 2013, SDNY (mail fraud; wire fraud, including defrauding a finance company on a loan — the loan count is ✔1 here, so just say "fraud"). 10 years; $20M forfeiture; $28.4M restitution. Served his sentence; deported 2021. "Counterfeiter", "fraud", "convicted" are all safe. He was the first person in the US tried and convicted for selling fake wine, per press at the time (✔1 — say "one of the biggest wine fraud cases ever", or skip).
- The auction house and the buyers were **victims/third parties** — no one else was convicted. Don't say the auctioneer "was in on it".

## On-screen-safe quotes
- Laurent Ponsot at trial (Dec 2013), about the pre-1982 bottles — Decanter's headline: **"This wine cannot exist."** The two trade reports attach the line to different vintages (Decanter: 1959 and 1962 bottles; Drinks Business: the 1945). Safe use: "The winemaker told the jury a bottle like this cannot exist." — paraphrase, no quote marks, until the Decanter page is opened.
- No other quotes.

## Commonly repeated but NOT verified here — don't say
- "He sold $550 million of fake wine" / "$130 million" / "10,000 of his bottles are still out there" — collector estimates, not court findings.
- "The biggest wine fraud in history" as flat fact → "one of the biggest".
- "Dr. Conti" nickname, "he drank $1 million of wine a month", "his uncles stole $800 million from Indonesian banks" — documentary/secondary lore.
- "Experts couldn't tell the difference" as an absolute — some raised doubts early. Say "plenty of experts drank it and bought more".
- Any exact count of Ponsot lots or their estimate ("$600,000").
- "19,000 labels" (one source) · the exact recipe ("he mixed Napa cabernet with old Bordeaux") — ✔1.
- "He's making wine again / hosting fake-wine dinners in Asia" — post-2021 press colour, not checked.
- Never mock the buyers by name; the roast target is the convicted forger and the "date on the label".
